A NOTE BEFORE YOU LISTEN

What follows is a broadcast booth that never existed, calling a game that isn't really a game. There's no real announcer and no real analyst. But everything they describe, every date, every file, every pull request, every test result, is real and comes straight from the project's commit history and its automated test runs. Only the booth is invented.

THE ONE PLAY THAT RAN

ANNOUNCER: Last night, 16 minutes before five Eastern, the founder himself steps onto the field, straight push to the main line of code. The target: DIG, the part of CiViX that checks where different news and advocacy sources stand on a bill and shows a citizen both sides.

ANALYST: DIG caches its answers for twenty-four hours, so it doesn't re-ask its research tool every time somebody looks at the same bill. But a cached answer used to show only the clock time it was first checked, something like two fourteen P.M. On its own, that number can't tell you whether it's from this morning or yesterday. Open DIG at nine A.M., see "two fourteen P.M.," and your brain reads that as later today, which makes no sense.

ANNOUNCER: So the fix: cached cards now say how old the answer actually is. "Saved five hours ago." Plain English, no math required. A dozen lines in the page itself, with a new test added alongside it, so if this ever breaks again, the test catches it before it ships.

ANALYST: That's the whole box score for the period. One play, well executed, tested on the way out. Both check-ins since then came back green, the run right after the push and last night's regularly scheduled nightly run, each clearing all three marks: the plain unit tests, the live check against the real production site, and the phone-layout check across every screen size CiViX supports.

ANNOUNCER: Clean. Nothing broken, nothing at risk from last night's work specifically.

THE BENCH REPORT

ANALYST: Now here's where I have to be the analyst who brings bad news to a good broadcast. There's a second team on this field, folks, and it's been playing the whole time without anybody from the sideline waving it in.

ANNOUNCER: You're talking about the nightly investigator.

ANALYST: I am. An automated routine CiViX built for itself back in September. Every night, right after the seven seventeen Eastern test run finishes, it checks the results. All green, it stands down. But if something failed, it finds out why, writes an actual fix, proves the fix with a test, and opens a pull request, a formal proposal sitting off on its own branch, waiting for a human to wave it onto the field.

ANNOUNCER: And how many of those are on the bench right now?

ANALYST: Six. Oldest first: October sixth, a failure that turned out to be a GitHub outage, not CiViX's own fault. October tenth, a real bug, named committees like the Select Committee on Intelligence were showing bills as simply "introduced" when they'd actually been read and referred, because the word "select" broke the pattern CiViX uses to read the government's own status text. October fifteenth, a false alarm over routine procedural language. October twentieth, missing headlines traced to the outside news service, not CiViX's code. October twenty-first, another false alarm. And yesterday morning, a crash in the tool that checks how CiViX's own pages look on a phone screen, nothing a citizen would ever see.

ANNOUNCER: Of those six, how many actually touch what a citizen sees?

ANALYST: One for certain, the October tenth committee-status bug. Right now, a citizen looking at a bill in front of the Select Committee on Intelligence or a similar named committee sees "introduced" when the honest answer is "already referred." Small, but exactly the kind of thing CiViX promises to get right, and the fix has been sitting, tested and ready, since the tenth. Nineteen days, as of this morning.

ANNOUNCER: Worth saying plainly: this same backlog got flagged in yesterday's brief too, in a different costume. One day later, it's the same six, unchanged, plus a seventh thing the investigator found and reported in again today.

ANALYST: Which is the system working as designed. It doesn't touch the main line of code on its own, on purpose, because an automated routine shipping its own fixes to a live civic platform with no human checking is a real risk. The tradeoff is exactly what we're watching: correctness sitting in a drawer until a person opens it.
