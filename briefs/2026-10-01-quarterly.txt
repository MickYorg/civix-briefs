CIVIX QUARTERLY BRIEF, OCTOBER 1


HOW TO LISTEN TO THIS

This is the quarterly brief for CiViX, written to be heard rather than read. It runs about forty minutes. The first half is the plain story of the quarter: what got built, where things honestly stand, and what's next. The second half is something different on purpose — a sea story, told as a ship's log, imagining two different futures for CiViX and ending with the real signs that would tell us which one we're actually sailing toward. Everything about the project itself — every feature, every bug, every date — is real and comes from the project's own commit history and notes. The log entries in the second half are invented on purpose, to make a point vividly, and I'll say so clearly when we get there.


WHAT CIVIX IS, FOR ANYONE JUST TUNING IN

CiViX is a civic engagement platform, on the web and as an iPhone and Android app. A citizen builds a private manifesto — not a profile, a manifesto, because it's meant to carry what they actually stand for — of the issues they care about. CiViX matches that manifesto against what's actually happening: bills moving through Congress, state legislatures, and city councils. Then it turns that match into something the citizen can actually do — a call script, an email, a real one-button send to a state legislator — rather than just another headline to feel bad about.

Two promises sit underneath everything else. First, that CiViX genuinely gets to know the citizen — not demographically, but in terms of what they actually care about and how they actually talk. Second, that it advocates for them effectively — turns that knowledge into real action, not just information. The project's own stated bar isn't "no bugs." It's that a citizen should find CiViX a richer, more useful experience than simply going and Googling a bill themselves. That's a high bar, and this quarter was largely about taking it seriously rather than treating it as a slogan.


THE STORY SO FAR, LIGHTLY

CiViX started life as something built mostly through conversations with Claude, Anthropic's AI assistant — pages and ideas built up in chat sessions before there was ever a formal code repository tracking changes over time. That changed on the twenty-fifth of August, when the project got real version control for the first time: a git repository, pushed to GitHub, with the working pages as they existed that day. Around the same time, the site finished moving its hosting from one cloud provider, Netlify, to another, Cloudflare — a real migration, not just a plan, verified line by line against the live site.

Through late August, the core features took shape. DIG, the part of CiViX that checks where different news sources stand on an issue and surfaces talking points for both sides of a debate, got built out and tied into the same manifesto data the rest of the app uses, so a citizen's trusted sources and the issues they care about are one shared thing instead of two separate lists drifting apart. The main flow for building a manifesto — swiping through real headlines, drilling into the ones that feel complicated, rating sources — came together. State governments got a real one-button way to email a legislator, through a service called OpenStates that actually publishes real legislator email addresses, something the federal equivalent, a nonprofit called 5calls, has never had. Municipal government data — city council agendas — got wired in for eight cities, through a tool called Legistar that several cities happen to use, though there's no single unified source for municipal data the way there is at the federal and state level.

Then came the tenth of September, and a turning point worth dwelling on, because it reset how the whole project gets worked on from here forward. The project's founder stepped back from fixing individual bug reports one at a time and named the real stakes directly: CiViX has those two core promises, and it wasn't yet reliably keeping either of them. The founder's own words were blunt — a citizen could get a richer experience just Googling a bill than from using an app that kept mis-hearing or mis-directing them. And there was a fair challenge buried in that feedback too: this kind of "go audit the app and find what's actually broken" request had been made before, more than once, and one-off patches to whatever bug happened to surface next clearly weren't enough on their own.

That session answered the challenge by actually walking the whole core loop end to end with real, live data — not just reading the code and assuming it worked, but using the app the way a citizen would and watching what actually came back. It found six real structural bugs, not cosmetic ones. Someone typing several different priorities into the freeform "anything else on your mind" box was having them silently collapsed into one lossy request, losing the ones that didn't survive. When the AI couldn't classify what a citizen meant, the failure got swallowed into a vague, unhelpful fallback instead of being handled honestly. A scoring bug let any unmatched, oddly specific priority unconditionally win a spot in the citizen's top three recommended actions — which is why the very same bill could show up or not show up depending on pure luck, a genuinely confidence-destroying kind of inconsistency. There was a brittle way of reading the AI's replies that broke if the model added so much as an unprompted aside. And there was a technical detail — certain web error codes that Cloudflare, the hosting provider, silently rewrites and discards the real message behind — found in the calendar feature and then confirmed lurking in nearly every other piece of backend code in the app, including the one powering DIG itself.

All six got fixed at the root, not patched at the symptom. And then, memorably, the exact same bug reappeared in a screenshot from the founder's own phone even after all six fixes were live — which turned into a genuinely good debugging story. The code was right. Cloudflare was serving the fixed JavaScript file with a four-hour browser cache, and there was no instruction in the project telling browsers not to do that. A fix could be correctly deployed and still be invisible on a real phone for up to four hours. That got fixed with a small configuration file. But even after that, the live production site still wasn't honoring the new no-cache instruction — turned out there was a separate, zone-level caching rule sitting on top of the one the app itself could control, something only the founder, not the code, could change. Once that was lowered, and once a stubborn phone browser tab that had cached the old, bad version was confirmed to still show the old result simply because it had never been told to check again — a private browsing tab, with no cache at all, showed the correct result immediately. That whole chain, from a six-bug audit to a four-layer caching mystery, is a fair example of the kind of discipline this project leaned into this quarter: verify what the citizen actually sees, live, not just that the code looks right.

That same discipline kept paying off. On the thirteenth of September, what started as chasing down an expired API key — the kind of credential that lets CiViX talk to Anthropic's AI models — turned into something bigger: a real, honest system for tracking exactly how much CiViX spends on AI, broken down by feature, with real token counts and real dollar figures, not estimates. The founder's framing made clear why this mattered beyond a vanity number: a transparent accounting of real AI spend is meant to eventually be part of the pitch for donations and institutional sponsorships, where organizations might literally donate usage credit to subsidize ordinary citizens. If that pitch is ever going to work, the numbers behind it have to be real and auditable, not a guess dressed up as a dashboard.

On the twentieth of September, two separate but connected decisions got made. First, after genuinely researching rather than guessing, the founder decided against expanding CiViX internationally for now — and specifically decided that if that ever gets revisited, the first country to try would be Canada, not France, because Canada has one unified data source covering federal, provincial, and municipal representatives from a single postal code lookup, which is a far closer match to CiViX's existing three-tier structure than anything available in France today.

Second, and much bigger: the decision to build toward a real initial release on Apple's App Store and the Google Play Store, using a technology called Capacitor, which wraps the existing website in a thin native shell rather than requiring a ground-up rewrite. Real push notifications and a native version of "Send to CiViX" — the feature that lets someone share something they read straight into the app — got built now rather than deferred, which was itself a reversal of an earlier decision to put off push notifications until there was dedicated infrastructure for them. But that reversal came with teeth: the founder insisted that the manifesto's own founding promise — never shared, never sold — had to be a literal engineering constraint on how push and sharing got built, not marketing copy applied afterward. Concretely, that meant push notification banners never name the actual bill or topic, since a notification can sit on a lock screen anyone might glimpse; that only the basic message-delivery piece of Google's Firebase service gets used, with none of its analytics or tracking bundled in; and that turning off notifications is a real deletion on the server, not a toggle that just hides something on the screen.

The three weeks that followed were, frankly, a toolchain adventure as much as a feature-building one. The development machine is an older Intel-based Mac, and both Apple's Xcode and the Homebrew package manager have been quietly dropping support for that kind of hardware in their newest releases. Getting the iPhone build pipeline working at all meant tracking down the last version of Xcode that still supports Intel chips, working around a Homebrew installer that now refuses to even run on anything but the newer Apple-silicon chips, and sidestepping a broken dependency in Homebrew's own Ruby programming-language package by installing a different, simpler version of Ruby directly. None of that is glamorous, and all of it is now written down so nobody has to solve it twice.

It was worth it. By the twenty-third of September, the iPhone version of the app was built, installed, and actually launched on Apple's simulator — not just compiled, but watched running, with the splash screen correctly flowing into the manifesto builder and the new native bottom navigation bar rendering the way it was designed to. That same real-world test immediately surfaced two more bugs that no amount of testing on a regular desktop browser could ever have caught: committee hearing locations displaying as garbled run-on text in the municipal events feature, and drafted phone-call scripts showing raw, unformatted leftovers from the AI's own reply instead of clean text meant to be read aloud on a real call. Both got fixed the same day, along with a subtler, genuinely interesting discovery: on a real iPhone, a browser request can come back looking successful but carrying a body that fails to parse, apparently because iOS throttles network activity for an app that isn't held strictly in the foreground — something that had never shown up in any of the project's prior testing, because that testing had only ever happened on a desktop, where this physically cannot occur.

The Android build came together the same week, on the emulator, and uncovered its own platform-specific landmine: the way the app's local web server was configured meant it was intercepting every single API call — every request for AI-driven data — and silently answering with the app's own home page instead of the real data, which meant every AI feature in the Android app was completely dead until that was found and fixed. The iPhone version, it turned out, had the mirror-image version of the same category of bug a day later: iPhone apps load their pages under a special internal address rather than a normal web address, which meant every one of those same relative API calls was being swallowed the same way, just for a different underlying reason. Both are now fixed, and both are documented clearly enough that if a third platform-specific gotcha of this shape shows up, there's a playbook for finding it fast.

By the twenty-fourth of September, enough of this had landed that the founder declared a phase of work finished and named it: Streamlining. That's the shorthand for a bundle of infrastructure that's easy to take for granted once it exists and was genuinely absent before: a single command to start a coding session, a real automated test suite that runs in seconds, a safety check that blocks pushing broken code before it ever reaches the live site, a continuous-integration pipeline on GitHub that reruns everything on every change and also every night, a nightly investigator routine that specifically watches for anything that pipeline catches overnight and fixes it on its own branch rather than touching the main line of code directly, a path toward testing real builds through Apple's TestFlight program, and — not incidentally — the very system of scheduled listening briefs that produced the words you're hearing right now.

The last week of the quarter, running right up through today, has mostly been about one feature getting rebuilt properly: Send to CiViX. The old version had a whole separate inbox page with its own adopt-or-reject list, which has now been retired entirely. In its place, every single thing sent to CiViX — a share from an iPhone, a paste, an email — gets a real server-side "dig": CiViX reads what was sent, and, using Claude with live web search, works out a plain headline, a summary, the real underlying bill if one actually exists, fact-checked claims, up to three ranked possible moves, and real sources. None of that is guessed or faked; if a specific bill can't be confirmed, the system doesn't pretend one exists. Each dig's topic now actually feeds the citizen's manifesto, raising its priority the more it keeps coming up, rather than just sitting in a dead-end list. A real push notification — carrying only a generic "CiViX looked into what you sent" message, consistent with that trust-first rule from a week earlier — tells the citizen when a dig is ready, and tapping it opens the exact item, not just the app in general.

Fixing all of that surfaced one more structural finding worth naming out loud: two Cloudflare Workers — small, separate background programs this project runs — tried to call each other directly over the public internet and got a silent, confusing failure every time, because same-account Workers calling each other's public addresses simply doesn't work on that platform; they have to use something called a service binding instead, a more direct internal connection. That one bug meant every "CiViX dug in" push notification had been silently failing to send since the feature existed, until it was found and fixed on the twenty-sixth of September.

Alongside all of that, DIG's privacy promise got sharpened into something closer to literal: searches are now counted publicly by broad topic — "housing," say — rather than by the actual words a citizen typed, and anything under five occurrences, or anything containing profanity, slurs, or personal details, simply doesn't show up in the public statistics at all. And, in something of a side project, an experimental page called Source Map got built: a drag-and-drop board that plots DIG's forty-some news sources by trustworthiness and leaning, including who actually funds and owns each outlet, cross-checked against real financial filings and reporting. It isn't linked from anywhere in the live app yet — it's an experiment sitting in the repository, waiting to earn its place.


WHERE THINGS HONESTLY STAND TODAY

As of this morning, the automated test pipeline is green. It wasn't always green this quarter — there was a real stretch, the twenty-seventh and twenty-eighth of September, where several pushes in a row broke the nightly build, over a batch of advocacy-data fixes and a layout check that opens real action modals. The nightly investigator routine caught it, root-caused it to a button that had quietly shrunk below the minimum comfortable tap size once real representative data made some labels longer than the placeholder data ever had, fixed it, and the suite's been clean since. That's actually a good sign rather than a bad one — it's the safety net doing exactly the job it was built for, catching something before a citizen ever saw it.

The core promise — manifesto, matched to the civic calendar, turned into action — is genuinely proven end to end for federal legislation, and the matching half of that loop now covers state legislation too, with a real one-button send for state. Municipal coverage is real now, not sample data, but only for eight cities, because there's no single nationwide source for city council information the way there is federally and by state — each new city means finding and verifying that city's own system by hand. Zip-code-based lookup of a citizen's representative is best-effort, since a zip code doesn't map perfectly onto a congressional district; a full address would be more precise, and the app deliberately doesn't ask for one except in the narrow, already-consented moment of actually sending a state email.

The most honest permanent gap in the whole app is the federal "send" feature. For a state legislator, CiViX can send a real email with one tap, because the organization that supplies state legislator data happens to publish real email addresses. No equivalent public source of real congressional staff email addresses exists for the federal level, and the project has made a firm, deliberate decision never to solve that by having CiViX fill out a government contact form on a citizen's behalf, because those forms are protected by exactly the kind of bot-detection the project will not try to defeat, under any justification. So federal contact, for the foreseeable future, ends in a drafted script or email that the citizen copies and sends themselves. That's named here specifically because it would be easy to quietly treat it as solved when it isn't.

A couple of smaller known issues are sitting in the open, honestly flagged rather than hidden: a test citizen's top three recommendations once included a bill about a military surveillance drone, tagged under "Housing affordability" — clearly a false match from loose keyword matching that hasn't been traced down yet. Some small text labels on the more advanced Pro mode and on the public analytics page are still smaller than they should be on a phone screen — allowed for now by the project's own layout rules, but flagged to shrink that allowance over time rather than get comfortable with it. And the daily shared budget for how much CiViX spends on DIG checks across all citizens combined hasn't been raised to match a recent five-times increase in how many checks any one visitor is allowed to run themselves — which means, in theory, enough simultaneous real use could hit the shared ceiling before any individual hits their own.


THE NEXT FEW WEEKS

The most concrete near-term work is finishing the path to an actual store submission. That means auditing the "advocating effectively" side of the app with the same rigor the "getting to know the citizen" side already got back on the tenth of September — the municipal events feature, the representative lookups, the docket classification — since it was explicitly flagged as not yet checked that carefully. It means writing an actual Privacy Policy and Terms of Service, neither of which exists yet; a store submission simply can't go out without them. It means filling in Apple's and Google's privacy questionnaires accurately, and preparing real store listing text and screenshots — which is, not coincidentally, exactly what that automated demo-clip recording work from the last week of the quarter was quietly building toward: a repeatable way to produce a short, honest video of the app actually working, taps and all. And it means getting Android into real internal testing on Google Play, which is currently blocked on something mundane: there's no Android device to test on yet, only an emulator. Apple-side testing, through TestFlight, is further along, since there is a real iPhone connected to this process already.


THE NEXT QUARTER

Beyond the store submission itself, a real list exists, named by the founder back in September and intentionally not acted on yet, so it isn't lost: a lighter-weight version of DIG's stance-checking surfaced directly inside the simple citizen flow, rather than requiring someone to find DIG's own separate page; a real path for a citizen to graduate from the simple "citizen" mode into the more hands-on "activist" mode as their engagement grows, rather than the one-time choice that exists today; a way to actually spend the CiViX Coin that citizens can already earn through quizzes, since right now there's no spending side to that loop at all; an actual donation and sponsorship mechanism, building on top of this quarter's new, real spend-transparency dashboard; a more fleshed-out mockup of what a Pro or organizational version of CiViX could look like; and, discussed but deliberately not started, a "Hey CiViX" voice feature — which, worth noting honestly, turned out to split into at least three genuinely different ideas once the founder talked it through: speaking a priority instead of typing it, having a drafted call script read aloud for rehearsal before a citizen actually dials, and a true voice-assistant that answers spoken questions about what's happening on a bill. Any real version of that last one would need to process speech entirely on the device itself, never sending raw audio anywhere, to hold the same trust line the rest of the app now holds by design. International expansion stays explicitly shelved, with Canada marked as the honest first choice if and when that changes.


THAT'S THE STORY OF THE QUARTER

A project that started as conversations in a chat window got real version control, moved hosting providers, built out its core loop, got audited hard and found honestly wanting, fixed what it found at the root, built real financial transparency into its own AI spending, chose deliberately not to go international yet, committed to a real app store release and spent three weeks fighting an aging laptop to get there, found and fixed three separate platform-specific bugs that only existed because the app was finally running on real phones instead of a desktop browser, built a real testing and nightly-investigation safety net, and closed out the quarter rebuilding its most-used feature, Send to CiViX, into something that actually digs into whatever a citizen sends it rather than just filing it away. That's a lot of real, verified progress. It's also, honestly, still a young project with real limits — eight cities, one country, a permanent gap at the federal "send" step, and a citizen base small enough that there's genuinely only one real day of spend-history data to look at yet.

Which brings us to the second half.


A NOTE BEFORE THE SECOND HALF

What you're about to hear is not a forecast. It's a deliberate piece of imagination, in the form of a ship's log — fitting, since a fair amount of this project's own work happens from a boat. Every project fact inside it is real, drawn from everything you just heard. Every voice, every entry, every future event is invented to make a point vividly, and I'll keep marking that clearly as we go, because the whole purpose of imagining two futures is to think honestly about which signs would actually tell us which one we're heading toward — not to pretend either one has already happened.


TWO FUTURES, LOGGED AT SEA

What follows is a log that has not been written yet, kept by an imagined future version of CiViX's founder, somewhere out on open water, thinking back over what happened and what didn't. There are two versions of this log. Read them both as fiction built from real facts, not as a prediction.


LOG, FIRST VOYAGE — FAIR WINDS

Entry, a year from now, late at night, at anchor. We crossed a real number today — not a vanity metric, a hard one, pulled off the spend dashboard this project built back in September of last year: a hundred thousand manifestos, built by real citizens, each one actually matched against something moving through a city council, a statehouse, or Congress. I keep thinking about how small that dashboard looked when it shipped — one day of history, a bar chart with a single bar. It's a long chart now.

The thing that actually tipped it, if I'm honest, wasn't a feature. It was trust, held for long enough that people noticed. Never shared, never sold stopped being a line in a planning document the week a journalist actually tried to break it — ran every test she could think of, found the push notifications really do say nothing but "open the app," found the opt-out really does delete the server record, not just hide a toggle — and wrote that up instead of a takedown. After that, the kind of citizen who'd normally assume any free civic app is secretly selling their political leanings to someone started showing up anyway. Word of mouth did the rest, slower than anyone wanted and faster than anyone expected, the way it always seems to.

The federal "send" gap never did get solved the clever way — still no congressional staff email list, still no bot-and-CAPTCHA-defeating form-filler, because we never built one and never will. What happened instead is almost funnier: enough citizens kept copy-pasting CiViX's own drafted calls into actual phone calls that congressional offices started recognizing the voice of the thing, the plain, no-throat-clearing tone this project taught it to write in in September. Never officially confirmed, obviously, but one state legislator's staffer told a citizen directly that they could tell when a constituent call "came from that app." Make of that what you will.

Canada went first, the way it was always going to, because the data fit before anyone forced it to. The Pro and organizational mockup turned into something real too — a handful of advocacy nonprofits paying to run shared manifestos for their own members, their usage credits literally subsidizing ordinary citizens the way the September dashboard always said they were building toward. Voice shipped in its smallest, most honest form first: reading a drafted script aloud before a call, on-device, nothing sent anywhere. The wake-word version never did happen — turned out nobody really missed it once the quieter version existed.

Here's the part worth saying plainly, because a future where this works isn't automatically a clean one. A hundred thousand manifestos is also a hundred thousand private pictures of exactly what worries people, sitting in one place, and "never shared, never sold" is a promise this project has to keep making true forever, not just the day it's tested. Real reach also means real targets: a project that genuinely moves votes becomes a project someone, eventually, will try to capture, co-opt, or discredit, and a platform this trusted could do real harm the one time it gets that trust wrong, by a bad match, a bad draft, a bias nobody caught. Winning doesn't retire the stakes. It raises them.


LOG, SECOND VOYAGE — BECALMED

Entry, also a year from now, also at anchor, a different night. Nothing sank. That's almost the whole problem.

The honest post-mortem, if I had to write one, doesn't have a single villain in it. The store submission went out, eventually, months later than the "one to two more weeks" this quarter's notes once hoped for — Google's own mandatory testing window for a new developer account ran longer than planned, and the advocacy-side audit turned up two more structural bugs exactly like the ones found back in September, because that's apparently just what happens every time this project points its own hard-won "verify live, don't assume" standard at a part of itself it hadn't checked yet. Each one got fixed properly. Each one cost a week. Weeks add up.

Once it was out, it was fine. Reviews were fine. Nobody complained about the eight-city municipal coverage, because nobody outside those eight cities ever really tried it hard enough to notice. The thing that never happened was the thing that was never going to show up in any bug report: a citizen opening the app, matching it against a real bill, drafting a real call, and feeling like CiViX understood something about them that Google search didn't — often enough, and shared widely enough, for one friend to tell another. The bar this project set for itself in September — richer than just Googling it — is a bar you can clear quietly, one person at a time, and still never cross the harder threshold of people choosing to talk about it. Nobody's manifesto went anywhere. Not because it was taken from them. Because too few people ever filled one out to begin with.

The federal "send" gap, the one this project decided on principle never to paper over with a CAPTCHA-breaking shortcut, turned out to matter more than hoped. A lot of the citizens who tried CiViX were there specifically because of something federal — a bill in Congress, not a city council agenda — and "copy this into an email yourself" is one extra step that a real, sizeable number of people simply didn't take. Word spread, a little, that CiViX was "the app that still makes you do the last part yourself," which isn't wrong, exactly, and wasn't fair either, but perception doesn't negotiate.

Underneath both of those: the project never did get past being mostly one person's late nights and a Claude session, some of it genuinely written up from a boat with good satellite internet and nothing but time. That was never going to be a weakness by itself — plenty of real things start that way — but it meant every week spent on toolchain archaeology, or an Intel Mac fighting a build tool that had quietly dropped support for it, was a week not spent on the one thing that might have actually moved the needle: making a stranger, with no reason yet to trust a civic app, feel something in the first ninety seconds. Nothing failed loudly. It just never quite caught, and the boat kept moving, and so did the calendar.

A quieter future is not automatically a wasted one, even in this version. A small, well-built, honestly-run civic tool that only ever serves a few thousand people who genuinely trust it is still worth more than it cost to build. It's just not the future the splash page was written for.


THE SIGNALS

Those are two made-up logs. Here is what's real: the actual, checkable things worth watching over the next several months that would tell, in advance, which of those two directions this is actually drifting toward — not predictions, just the honest tells.

Whether a citizen who finishes building a manifesto actually comes back a second time without being pushed to. That's the single clearest sign of whether the thing itself is working, independent of how many people ever hear about it in the first place.

Whether the federal "send" gap stays a named, honest limitation or quietly becomes the reason people stop recommending the app to a friend. Worth asking real citizens directly, not guessing.

Whether municipal coverage actually grows past eight cities in the next few months, or stalls there, because growing it one city at a time is exactly the kind of unglamorous work that's easy to deprioritize in favor of whatever's more exciting that week.

Whether the daily-spend dashboard, a year from now, shows a chart with real shape to it — growth, or at least a real baseline — rather than staying the single flat bar it still mostly is today.

Whether the trust constraints this project put in writing in September — the bill never named in a push notification, the opt-out that's a real delete, no bundled analytics — hold up the first time someone genuinely tries to find a crack in them. That test will come, in some form, whether or not it's ever written up this dramatically.

And, maybe most simply: whether the store submission actually goes out in the next few weeks, on something close to the current plan, or whether it keeps sliding a quarter at a time the way ambitious software timelines tend to. Not because the deadline itself matters, but because what pushes a deadline usually says something true about where the real friction in a project actually lives.

None of that is dramatic. It's not supposed to be. It's just the honest list of things worth checking back on, out loud, the next time this brief comes around.

That's the quarter.
